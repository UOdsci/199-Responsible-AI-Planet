Responsible Use of AI for Planetary Futures

AI can help us better monitor, manage, and protect a changing planet. But AI creates new challenges for environmental sustainability and the conservation workforce.

What is this course about?

Artificial intelligence is transforming how people monitor and map biodiversity, and how we make environmental decisions. At the same time, AI systems can require copious amounts of energy, water, minerals, labor, and data. AI systems can widen access to knowledge but deepen inequity.

In this class, we will ask a deceptively simple question: what would it mean to use AI responsibly on a living planet? This is a fast-moving field where students can and should have a voice on how these systems impact our shared commons and future; there are no right answers, and we are going to collectively learn about the fundamentals of AI systems, examine environmental applications, investigate ethical and social concerns, and proactively think through what responsible use and governance would look like. Our goal is to help model for the conservation sector what it would look like to make better decisions with AI together.

You do not need prior experience in data science, computer science, or environmental studies. Bring your questions, your perspective, and a willingness to take leadership over your own learning journey!

What does AI actually “know”?

We will unpack how models learn from data, where uncertainty enters, and why outputs can sound more certain than the evidence allows.

What does intelligence cost?

We will follow the material life of AI through electricity, water, mining, and other environmental concerns.

Who gets to decide?

We will examine power, participation, Indigenous data sovereignty, governance, and the communities affected by automated decisions.

Can AI serve a thriving planet?

We will imagine uses shaped by ecological limits, justice, and public purpose.

Learning goals

By the end of the course, a successful learner will be able to:

  1. Explain foundational ideas about AI in clear language.
  2. Trace the environmental and social impacts of an AI system across its life cycle.
  3. Evaluate claims about AI for climate, conservation, and sustainability using evidence rather than hype.
  4. Identify whose knowledge, labor, and interests are represented or excluded in an AI-enabled decision.
  5. Communicate a practical governance recommendation to a real-world audience.

How we will learn

Class meetings mix short lectures, discussions based on close reading (student participation is an essential part of this class!), small-group inquiry (you all are the AI and conservation workforce of the future), structured debate, and hands-on investigations. Some weeks we will inspect datasets or interact with AI systems, but this is not a programming course. The emphasis is on asking better questions of technical systems and thinking through what responsible choices would entail.

Signature group project

An AI governance field guide for conservation

Teams will examine how environmental and conservation organizations describe responsible and generative AI use. You will compare policies, identify important gaps, and develop a concise, usable field guide for an organization considering AI in its work. Ideally, the field guide will focus on a practical need in environmental conservation.

Weekly project accountability. Your participation grade is not just about talking in class. Each week your team will log a small, concrete step toward the group field guide (a source found, a policy compared, a section drafted, a decision made). These weekly check-ins keep the major project moving and give everyone a visible, low-stakes way to contribute. A range of participation counts–speaking in full-group and small-group discussion and your weekly project contributions–so there is more than one way to show up well.

Deadlines and late work

Assignments are due at the times listed. Late submissions will result in a grade penalty (one letter grade deduction per day late). But, I have built in some flexibility so a hard week does not derail you:

  • Each student has one “grace” extension–turn in a homework up to a week late, for any reason, no need to explain. (These do not apply to course assessments, or the group briefing, which are tied to fixed class dates.)
  • Your lowest homework score is adjusted. At the end of the term, I will drop the lowest scoring question on the lowest scoring homework for all students and increase your score accordingly.

Late work will receive reduced and/or delayed feedback because we will have moved on to new material. If you find yourself falling behind, reach out early. I would much rather plan a path forward with you proactively.

Our AI practice

Because responsible AI use is the subject of the course, we will sometimes use AI tools deliberately—and sometimes set them aside. Each assignment will name an AI use level:

Level Expectation
Observe Do not use generative AI; the purpose is to notice your own reasoning or build a foundational skill.
Consult Use AI for limited support such as brainstorming or critique, then verify, revise, and disclose what you used.
Collaborate AI use is part of the task; you will note down your interactions with the model and log its traces.

In every case, you remain responsible for claims, citations, decisions, and submitted work. We will develop our shared disclosure practice in the first weeks and revisit it together.

Attendance and engagement

Because this is a discussion seminar, our learning happens together, in the room. Your presence and preparation are the engine of the course, which is why participation and weekly project accountability count toward your grade.

You may miss up to two class meetings this term for any reason, no explanation needed. I do not distinguish between “excused” and “unexcused” absences–there is no fair way to verify anyone’s reasons–so these are yours to use as you see fit, whether you are ill, overwhelmed, or simply need a day. Please save them for when you truly need them. (As in all UO courses, absences for religious observances, AEC-approved accommodations, military service, and University-sponsored events are handled separately and do not count against this total; let me know as early as you can, and at least a week ahead when the absence is planned.)

If you are feeling ill, please stay home, rest, and use one of your absences. Protecting your classmates (and your own health!) matters more than perfect attendance.

Students who miss three or more meetings will not earn full participation credit, and in cases of extensive absence may fail the course. If something in your life is making attendance hard, please reach out. I would much rather build a plan with you early. Your success genuinely matters to me, and our learning community.

Learning together

The questions in this course do not have purely technical answers. Our classroom will include different experiences, values, and levels of confidence with both technology and environmental issues. We will critique ideas and systems without dismissing people; make room for uncertainty; explain unfamiliar terms; and treat changing one’s mind as evidence of learning.

All of us in this class–including me–can expect one another to:

  • Participate and contribute. Come prepared, follow the plan for the day, and engage thoughtfully. Participation is not limited to talking; asking questions, listening closely, building on a classmate’s idea, and your weekly work all count.
  • Expect and respect diversity. This class welcomes a range of experiences, perspectives, and approaches. We condemn behavior that demeans or marginalizes others on the basis of race, ethnicity, gender, sex, age, sexual orientation, religion, ability, or socioeconomic status. We can disagree with respect and evidence.
  • Help everyone learn. We learn from one another, which takes patience, generosity, and openness to feedback. Please ask for help when you need it and offer it when you can.
  • Hold mutual respect. We will respect one another, and that also means that we will respect each other’s right to privacy. What’s shared in class stays in class.

We will develop more specific shared norms together in the first weeks of the term.

If any aspect of the course creates a barrier to your participation, please speak with the instructor. We can work together on access needs, learning conditions, or connections to the appropriate campus resources. You do not need to wait for a problem to become urgent.
